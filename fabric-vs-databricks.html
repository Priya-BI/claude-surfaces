Fabric or Databricks

The question is normally asked as “which one handles large data better?” That question has no answer, because neither meter measures your data. This compares the two on what actually sets the bill: how many hours a day the compute is busy, and what each platform does when it is busier than you paid for.

Written for the person who has to defend a platform decision in a steering meeting, and needs arithmetic rather than a preference.

01The question, restated

You asked which platform is more cost-effective for large data, and which stays reliable as the data grows. Both halves of that carry an assumption that is worth testing before any number goes on a slide.

Assumption one: data volume drives cost

It does not. Look at what the two meters actually count.

Fabric bills capacity units per hour. You buy an F SKU and you pay for every hour it exists. The meter runs at the same rate whether the lakehouse holds 5 GB or 5 PB. Databricks bills Databricks units per hour of compute consumed. That meter counts how long a cluster ran and how big it was. It does not read your row count either.

Data volume reaches the bill only twice, and both are small compared with compute. Storage is charged per gigabyte-month — 0.219 SEK in OneLake hot storage. And bigger data takes longer to scan, so jobs run longer. That second effect is real, but it is mediated: what you pay for is the extra minutes, not the extra bytes.

The variable that actually moves the invoice by an order of magnitude is duty cycle: the fraction of the hours in a month during which the compute is doing work. Section 05 puts a number on it.

Assumption two: growth threatens reliability

On neither platform is the first failure a reliability failure.

Fabric's answer to more load than you paid for is throttling. That is a documented, staged, deliberate governance behaviour, not a fault. Requests get delayed, then rejected, in a published order. The platform is working exactly as designed while your users see errors.

Databricks' answer to more load is to start more machines. Nothing degrades. The consequence arrives four weeks later, on the invoice.

Both platforms are reliable under growth. They fail in different currencies — Fabric in latency and rejected requests, Databricks in money. Which failure your organisation can absorb is a better decision input than any benchmark.
The alternative worth steelmanning

You framed this as either/or. The strongest case against that framing is that mirroring an Azure Databricks Unity Catalog into OneLake is generally available and copies no data. If that holds, the real decision is not which platform but which engine writes the curated tables and which one serves them — and those can be different vendors. Section 09 argues that case properly, including where it breaks.

02What each one actually is

Microsoft Fabric, in one paragraph

A single software-as-a-service product that bundles a data lake (OneLake), a Spark runtime, a T-SQL warehouse, pipelines, real-time streaming, and Power BI. You buy one pool of compute — a capacity — and every workload draws from it. Storage is one lake in Delta Parquet format, shared by every engine. Administration and identity are the same ones your organisation already uses for Microsoft 365.

Databricks, in one paragraph

A compute and governance platform that runs on cloud storage you already own. You create clusters and warehouses, they start, do work, and stop. Unity Catalog governs tables, files, models, and permissions across workspaces. Storage stays in your own cloud account. The bill is metered against compute consumed, and the cloud provider bills the underlying machines separately.

The important structural difference is not the feature list. It is who owns the storage account and whether compute is pooled or per-job. Everything in the rest of this page follows from those two.

03Two meters, two shapes

The clearest way to hold these two in your head is to stop comparing features and compare the shape of the two bills. One is a rectangle. The other is a silhouette of your own workload.

Why this matters more than it sounds

A fixed bill is not just a price. It is a forecastable price, and in many organisations a forecastable number that is 30% too high beats a variable number that averages lower. If your finance function cannot absorb a month that is triple the last one, that constraint outranks the arithmetic in section 05.

Two things about the reservation that are easy to miss

Three years buys no extra discount. The three-year reservation is 28,272.024 SEK per capacity unit, which is exactly three times the one-year price of 9,424.008. You are committing for longer at the same annual rate. Most Azure reservations pay you for the longer lock; this one does not, so there is no pricing reason to choose it over the one-year term.

The discount is on committed spend, not on consumption. Reserving an over-sized capacity is the idle-capacity trap from section 05 with twelve months of lock-in attached. Size it first, reserve it second.

The F64 cliff — the single biggest hidden cost

Below F64, every person who views Power BI content needs a paid Power BI Pro or Premium Per-User licence. At F64 and above, a viewer with a free licence and the viewer role can read the content. Microsoft states this directly in its licensing documentation.

So the Fabric cost curve is not smooth. For an organisation with a few hundred report readers, the per-viewer licences below F64 can exceed the capacity saving that justified staying below it. Model the viewer licences before you model the SKU.

05The break-even is a duty cycle, not a data volume

Read this before the arithmetic

A capacity unit and a Databricks unit are not comparable units of work. Nobody outside the two vendors' engineering teams can tell you how many CU-seconds equal a DBU-second, and it would vary by workload if they could. Every cross-platform figure below therefore compares bills, not throughput. It tells you what the same money buys on each meter. It does not tell you what the same job costs.

Working it out

Take an F64 on a one-year reservation as the anchor, and ask what the same money buys on the Databricks batch meter.

My arithmetic, not Microsoft's and not Databricks' F64, 1-year reserved  =  64 CU × 9,424.008 / 12  =  50,261.38 SEK a month Same money on Premium Jobs Compute  =  50,261.38 ÷ 2.8558  =  17,600 DBU-hours Spread across a 730-hour month  =  24.1 DBU running continuously Or as nightly batch over 30 nights  =  587 DBU-hours a night Minus the virtual machines, which Azure bills separately  =  less than that

So the F64 is worth having if — and only if — your workload would consume more than about 17,600 DBU-hours a month on Databricks. If it would consume 3,000, the F64 is nearly six times too big and you are buying idle hours.

The cost of idle, stated plainly

This is the finding that changes the most decisions, and it is arithmetic anyone can check.

What an unused capacity unit really costs F2, 1-year reserved  =  1,570.67 SEK a month for 2 CU × 730 h = 1,460 CU-hours If you use all of it  →  a CU-hour costs 1.076 SEK If you use a tenth of it  →  the same CU-hour costs 10.76 SEK If you use a twentieth  →  21.52 SEK — twenty times list
Idle capacity is the largest single cost on Fabric, and it never appears as a line on the invoice. It appears as a rate you are paying without noticing, because the invoice total does not move.

Databricks has the mirror-image failure, and it is just as easy to miss: a cluster left running with auto-termination disabled bills at the full rate while nobody uses it. The difference is that Databricks makes that visible — the cost goes up — whereas on Fabric the cost stays exactly the same and the waste hides inside it.

06One month, one company, both bills

Everything above is a shape. This section puts numbers in it, for one organisation, for one month. If you only read one part of this page, read this one — and then substitute your own figures, because the method matters more than my answer.

The company

A global lift manufacturer. Around 2,000 staff, four production plants, direct sales offices in 20 countries and distributors in about 70 more. It has no data platform today — reporting is spreadsheets and exports from the finance system. Year one is deliberately unglamorous: orders, invoices, inventory, customer records, field-service jobs and production bookings, landed once a night, modelled, and served to Power BI. No streaming. No machine learning. About 200 people who open a report, 15 who build them, and 4 engineers who run the whole thing.

This is a composite, not a real organisation, and every figure in it is mine. It is built to be typical rather than accurate.

First finding: the data is small

Before pricing anything, size it. This is the step most platform decisions skip, and it is the one that decides whether the rest of the conversation is even necessary.

How much data a global manufacturer actually has ~15,000 orders a year × ~30 lines  =  450,000 rows a year inventory movements  =  ~3,000,000 rows a year field-service jobs  =  ~200,000 rows a year production bookings  =  ~2,000,000 rows a year   total  ≈  5,650,000 fact rows a year three years of history  ≈  17,000,000 rows at ~250 bytes a row, uncompressed  ≈  4 GB × 4 for raw, cleaned, modelled and staging copies  ≈  17 GB

The modelled dataset for a global company with a nine-figure turnover is tens of gigabytes. Not terabytes, and nowhere near petabytes. That is not unusual — it is what transactional business data actually weighs once you stop counting log files.

The costing below allows 2 TB anyway, roughly a hundred times the modelled size, to cover raw file landings, document attachments, audit history, and everything nobody predicted. Even at that deliberate overshoot, storage never rises above about 1% of the bill. Storage is not the decision. It is a rounding error.

Look again at the table. The largest figure on it is not compute. It is the gap between two Fabric SKUs — and that gap exists to buy free report viewing, not processing power.

Choosing the size

The risk in the cheap option, named

F16's problem is not the nightly batch. It is 200 people opening reports at 09:00 on a Monday. The batch is comfortable — 32 Spark VCores, 96 with bursting, against tens of gigabytes. Report queries are a different animal: they are interactive operations, smoothed over only five to sixty-four minutes, and they are the first thing section 07's staged throttle delays and then rejects.

So the honest form of the cheap recommendation is: start at F16, and watch the throttling chart in the capacity metrics app for the first two months. If interactive delay starts appearing at the morning peak, that is the signal to scale — not the invoice.

What I would actually buy

  1. Nothing here is working hard. Tens of gigabytes and a two-hour batch do not stress either platform. The performance question everyone opens with is not the question.
  2. Databricks compute really is the cheapest line — 5,168.60 SEK against 13,013.65. It is also the smallest line, which is the point.
  3. The biggest number in the whole comparison is a licensing threshold, not compute. That makes the platform choice nearly free and the licensing choice expensive.
  4. The saving is about 94,000 SEK a year — 7,845.05 a month at the midpoint, and at most 11,430 a month if Databricks lands at the bottom of its range. That is the strongest form of the case against my recommendation, and it still does not cover a second vendor relationship, a second set of skills to hire for, a second support contract, and the seam between them. One engineer costs more than the saving.
Buy Fabric. Start at F16 with Pro licences for the people who genuinely open reports — not F64 on day one. Move to F64 when your viewer count passes 37,696 SEK divided by your Pro rate, or when the morning peak starts throttling. Revisit Databricks the first time someone asks for real machine learning, not before.

The case against this: a company that expects to do serious data science within two years is buying a skills base as well as a platform, and starting on Fabric means starting that clock later. If you believe that is your trajectory, weigh section 09 harder than this section.

07What happens when the data grows

Fabric: bursting, smoothing, then a staged throttle

Fabric does two generous things before it does a strict one. Bursting lets an operation temporarily use more compute than the SKU provides. Smoothing then spreads that consumption forward in time so the burst does not trigger a throttle: interactive operations are smoothed over five to sixty-four minutes, background operations over a full 24 hours. Fabric's own worked example notes that a background job consuming six times the compute available in the next ten minutes still does not throttle an F2, because the cost is spread across 2,880 half-minute timepoints.

When that runs out, the throttle arrives in published stages.

Future capacity consumedStageWhat a user sees
Up to 10 minutesOverage protectionNothing. The spike is absorbed.
10 to 60 minutesInteractive delayEvery interactive request waits 20 seconds before it starts.
60 minutes to 24 hoursInteractive rejectionInteractive requests are refused. Background jobs still run.
Over 24 hoursBackground rejectionEverything is refused until the debt is paid down.

Two details matter operationally and are easy to miss:

  • Work already running is never throttled. Only requests submitted after throttling begins are affected. Long jobs complete.
  • A throttled capacity rejects new Spark jobs rather than queueing them. Normally Spark jobs from pipelines and schedulers queue, first-in-first-out, and retry automatically — with a 24-hour queue expiry. In a throttled state that safety net is gone.

The error text is worth memorising, because it is what a data engineer will paste into a chat at 03:00: CapacityLimitExceeded, and for Spark specifically HTTP 430 TooManyRequestsForCapacity.

For the implementer

Concurrency on Fabric Spark is core-based, not job-count-based. One capacity unit maps to two Spark VCores, and bursting allows up to three times the purchased VCores. An F64 therefore provides 128 VCores, bursting to 384, with a queue limit of 64. If the pool is configured for all 384, a single job can take the lot — which is why capacity admins can disable job-level bursting on shared capacities to stop one notebook starving everyone else.

Databricks: it scales, and it bills

There is no equivalent shared ceiling. Clusters autoscale within the bounds you set; serverless products provision on demand. As data grows, jobs take longer or clusters grow wider, and the DBU count rises accordingly.

That is a genuine reliability advantage — and it is also why Databricks cost governance is a discipline rather than a setting. The controls are cluster policies, auto-termination, and budget alerts, and every one of them is something an administrator has to actively configure. Nothing stops an expensive query by default.

The honest summary of section 07

Fabric gives you a hard spending ceiling and a soft service level. Databricks gives you a hard service level and a soft spending ceiling. Growth does not break either platform — it moves along whichever of those two axes the platform left soft.

08Pros and cons, plainly

Microsoft Fabric

One product, one bill, one meter
In its favour
  • The bill is knowable a year ahead. For many finance functions that alone decides it.
  • Power BI is not integrated — it is the same product. Direct Lake removes the import-refresh cycle entirely.
  • Identity, tenant policy, sensitivity labels and audit are the ones your organisation already runs.
  • One storage layer in open Delta Parquet, readable by every engine including engines outside Fabric.
  • Low floor: an F2 reserved is 1,570.67 SEK a month, so a proof of concept is genuinely cheap to start.
  • Analysts and report authors need no new tooling, no cluster concepts, and no new login.
Against it
  • Idle capacity bills at the full rate. A lightly used capacity can cost ten times list per unit of work.
  • Every workload shares one pool. A runaway notebook can throttle the executive dashboard.
  • The F64 licensing cliff makes the cost curve step, not slope.
  • Right-sizing is a recurring management chore, not a one-time decision.
  • Fewer escape hatches for advanced machine learning and bespoke runtime work.
  • Rapid product change: preview features and consumption rates move faster than most governance processes.

Databricks

Compute you start, governance you configure
In its favour
  • You pay for work done. Idle costs nothing when auto-termination is on.
  • No shared ceiling, so one team's batch job cannot reject another team's query.
  • Strong ground for machine learning, streaming and large-scale engineering — this is the platform's origin.
  • Storage stays in your own cloud account, which simplifies some data-residency and exit arguments.
  • Unity Catalog governs tables, files, and models under one permission model across workspaces.
  • Runs on more than one cloud, which matters if multi-cloud is a real constraint rather than a slide.
Against it
  • The bill is variable and can surprise. Nothing stops an expensive query by default.
  • Two bills, not one: Databricks units plus the cloud provider's machines.
  • Premium tier roughly doubles the batch rate, and regulated organisations usually need it.
  • Business-intelligence serving for a large audience normally still means Power BI, and therefore a second product and a second licence.
  • Cost discipline requires active administration — cluster policies, budgets, and someone whose job it is.
  • Steeper skills floor. Analysts need more platform concepts before they are productive.

09Choosing

Four questions, asked in this order. The order is the point: the first one that returns a clear yes should settle it, because it is the one with the largest financial consequence.

The last box is not a cop-out

If you reach the bottom, the platforms genuinely are close enough that the deciding factor is which one your team can operate on a bad day. A well-run Fabric estate beats a badly-run Databricks estate and the reverse is equally true. Hiring and existing skills are a legitimate technical input, not a soft one.

10The option you did not ask about

The either/or framing is worth attacking, because the strongest structural argument in this whole comparison is that you may not have to choose.

Mirroring an Azure Databricks Unity Catalog into Fabric is generally available. Microsoft's own documentation is unusually plain about the mechanics: “there is no data movement or data replication. Only the Azure Databricks catalog structure is mirrored to Fabric and the underlying catalog data is accessed through shortcuts.” Fabric creates a read-only T-SQL analytics endpoint over it, and Power BI can query it in Direct Lake mode.

The reverse direction is being built too. Databricks has published support for reading OneLake data through Unity Catalog federation, again read-only and without copying, with write support described as still under development.

So the architecture that dissolves the question looks like this: Databricks engineers the curated tables; Fabric serves them to the business. Each engine does what it is strongest at, and no gigabyte is copied to make it work.

Where that argument breaks

I would not present it without these four caveats, all of which are documented limits rather than opinions.

  • It is one-directional today. Fabric reads Databricks tables. Databricks writing directly into OneLake is not there yet. Your write path still has to live on one side.
  • Not everything mirrors. Materialized views and streaming tables are not shown, and external tables that are not in Delta format are not shown either. If your gold layer leans on those, the seam leaks.
  • It is not instant. Microsoft states propagation takes anywhere from a few seconds to several minutes. That is fine for reporting and wrong for anything operational.
  • You still pay both bills. Zero-copy removes the storage duplication and the pipeline, not the two platform subscriptions. This is an architecture argument, not a cost-saving one.
Run both only if you have a real reason for both — genuine machine learning work and a large Power BI audience. Two platforms because nobody would decide is the most expensive outcome on this page.

11Where this could be wrong

The parts of this comparison most likely to fail under scrutiny, listed so you can attack them first.

  • The prices are one region on one day. Everything came from swedencentral on 2026-09-05. The regional spread is not hypothetical: the same Fabric reservation is 15.8% dearer in West Europe, so an organisation that standardised on Amsterdam before Sweden Central existed is reading the wrong column of this page.
  • These are SEK list prices, not your prices. Enterprise agreements are negotiated, and they are negotiated in a currency. Nothing here accounts for a discount, and nothing here is an exchange-rate calculation — every figure is Microsoft's published kronor rate on the day.
  • List prices are not what large customers pay. Databricks committed-use discounts are not public, and Microsoft enterprise agreements are negotiated. Every Databricks figure here is undiscounted; the Fabric reservation discount is public and is included. That asymmetry flatters Fabric.
  • Capacity units and Databricks units are not commensurable. Section 05 says this outright, but it bears repeating: I have compared bills, not work. A real answer needs your workload benchmarked on both, which nobody wants to fund.
  • The clean “ceiling versus meter” split is eroding. Fabric now offers on-demand billing for Spark, where jobs run on separate serverless resources, consume no capacity units from the capacity, and are billed pay-as-you-go. Bursting and smoothing do not apply in that mode. That is a meter inside Fabric, and it weakens the tidiest argument on this page.
  • The Databricks-unit count per machine is unsourced. Section 06's Databricks column depends on how many units a virtual machine draws, and I could not find that published in a form I could verify. The figures in circulation differ by more than a factor of two, which is why that column is a range. It is the weakest number on the page.
  • The worked example is a composite. The manufacturer in section 06 is invented to be typical — no real organisation's headcount, data volumes or costs appear anywhere on this page. If your row counts differ by an order of magnitude, redo the arithmetic rather than borrowing the conclusion.
  • The 200-viewer figure carries the whole recommendation. Section 06 lands just below the licensing threshold, which is what makes it close-run. At 500 viewers the answer flips to F64 without any of the other arithmetic changing.
  • I have run no benchmarks. Every performance statement here is drawn from documented limits — VCore counts, burst factors, concurrency ceilings — not from measurement.
  • The Databricks side is characterised from fewer first-party pages than the Fabric side. Microsoft's throttling and licensing documentation is exceptionally detailed and public. The Databricks equivalents are thinner in public, so the Fabric half of this comparison is better evidenced than the Databricks half. Weigh accordingly.
